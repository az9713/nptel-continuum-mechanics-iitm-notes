Linear fluids, Navier–Stokes and a complete flow problem

A film flowing down an inclined surface has a velocity profile set by gravity, viscosity and two different boundary conditions: no slip at the solid and negligible tangential stress at the free surface. Finding that profile also requires its pressure field. The calculation joins the definitions, tensor algebra, conservation laws and material model developed throughout the course. A pressure-driven channel with a moving wall supplies a second, original comparison case.

Lecture spine: L57, fluid statics and the Newtonian stress; L58, Navier–Stokes equations and special cases; L59, the falling film. The three full machine transcripts were read sequentially. The film reproduces the lecture problem’s stated geometry, while its numerical parameters, power checks, channel comparison, code and exercises are original teaching constructions. The ideal laminar solution is not a stability or experimental-validation claim.

Fluid at rest and isotropic stress L57 1:46

Use fixed Cartesian coordinates x (m), time t (s), velocity v (m s⁻¹), and mass density ρ (kg m⁻³). Cauchy stress σ and pressure p have units Pa; positive p denotes compression. For a dimensionless unit outward normal n, the traction is t(n)=σn, with σij denoting force component i on a plane whose normal is in direction j. In the ordinary fluid model used here, a fluid at rest carries no shear traction on any plane. Its traction is therefore parallel to n for every unit normal. This condition is stronger than zero off-diagonal entries in one selected basis. A diagonal stress with unequal entries has shear traction on some oblique planes, even though its coordinate-face shear components vanish.

To derive the consequence, let σe1=a1e1, σe2=a2e2 and σe3=a3e3 by testing coordinate normals. Testing n=(e1+e2)/√2 requires (a1e1+a2e2)/√2 to be parallel to n, so a1=a2. A second mixed normal gives a2=a3. Thus σ=aI. Defining compressive pressure p=−a gives σ=−pI and traction t=−pn. The derivation uses the absence of shear on every plane and Cauchy’s linear traction formula.

Let the vector g be gravitational acceleration (m s⁻²), and let g>0 denote its magnitude. Static momentum under gravity is 0=−∇p+ρg. With z upward, g=−g ez, where ez is the upward unit vector, so dp/dz=−ρg. For constant density this integrates to p(z)=p(z0)−ρg(z−z0). The integration constant is fixed by a pressure boundary condition. A uniform pressure contributes no net force on a closed material element, while it can exert nonzero local force on every part of its surface.

The tensile-positive stress convention permits negative gauge pressure without implying a negative absolute thermodynamic pressure. Some liquids can also be placed in metastable tension; the simple statement that all fluids categorically cannot support any tension is broader than this ideal stress derivation establishes. The chapter uses a single-phase continuum model over a stipulated range and does not predict cavitation thresholds.

Stress of a linear viscous fluid L57 14:19

Define the rate-of-deformation tensor by Dij=(∂vi/∂xj+∂vj/∂xi)/2 (s⁻¹), so div v=tr D. The identity tensor is I, and the deviatoric part is Ddev=D−(div v)I/3. A compressible isotropic Newtonian law is σ=−pI+2μD+λv(div v)I, with dynamic viscosity μ and second viscosity λv, both in Pa s. Let Kvis=λv+2μ/3 denote bulk viscosity. The double contraction A:B=ΣiΣj AijBij gives viscous dissipation 2μ Ddev:Ddev+Kvis(div v)² in W m⁻³. Nonnegative μ and Kvis guarantee nonnegative viscous dissipation. Stokes’ hypothesis λv=−2μ/3 sets bulk viscosity to zero; it is an additional approximation, not a consequence of mass conservation.

The quantities μ, λv and Kvis have units Pa s. Here p is the thermodynamic pressure in a compressible constitutive description. Mechanical pressure is defined from stress as pm=−trσ/3. Taking the trace of the Newtonian law gives pm=p−Kvis divv. Consequently thermodynamic and mechanical pressure agree under Stokes’ hypothesis or when divv=0. An experiment with zero divergence cannot identify the bulk viscosity from this relation.

Viscous stress is not generally the deviatoric part of total stress. Its trace is3Kvis divv. Only after an additional restriction makes this trace vanish can the two be identified. This distinction is important in compressible motion. The lecture’s progression from two viscosity coefficients to a traceless viscous stress uses Stokes’ hypothesis; that step must not be mistaken for a universal tensor identity.

The stress law describes resistance to rates of deformation, not the only possible cause of motion. Pressure gradients and body forces can accelerate an inviscid fluid even when viscous shear stress is absent. Likewise, an already moving fluid can translate rigidly with D=0. A physical explanation should distinguish forces, acceleration and constitutive resistance rather than say that viscous shear is required for all fluid motion.

The differential equations L58 1:56

Let b be body force per unit mass (m s⁻²), so ρb is force per unit volume (N m⁻³); gravity alone gives b=g. The material acceleration is Dv/Dt=∂tv+(v·∇)v. For constant μ and λv, substituting stress into momentum gives ρDv/Dt=−∇p+μ∇²v+(μ+λv)∇(div v)+ρb. Variable viscosity produces additional gradient terms and cannot be pulled through a derivative. For incompressible constant-density flow, div v=0 and the equation reduces to ρ(∂tv+v·∇v)=−∇p+μ∇²v+ρb. Pressure enforces the velocity constraint and requires compatible boundary and reference information.

In components, differentiate the constitutive law using the force-component-first convention:

\[ \partial_j\sigma_{ij}=-\partial_i p +\mu\partial_j\partial_jv_i +(\mu+\lambda_v)\partial_i\partial_jv_j. \tag{15.1} \]

The second symmetric-gradient contribution becomes μ∂i∂jvj after commuting the mixed derivatives. The volumetric term contributes λv∂i∂jvj. These two pieces combine; they do not both vanish merely because the flow is steady. If μ varies spatially, product differentiation also gives (∂jμ)(∂jvi+∂ivj). For variable λv there is a further term (∂iλv)divv. Keeping a divergence-form stress operator is often the safest representation before simplifying coefficients.

For an isothermal compressible model, mass, momentum and an equation of state can form a closed mechanical subsystem. For incompressible motion, p is instead an unknown enforcing the divergence constraint and is generally not obtained by substituting a fixed density into an ordinary compressible equation of state. Fixing temperature does not claim that viscous dissipation vanishes; a thermal mechanism must remove or redistribute the generated energy if the temperature is to remain fixed.

Counting equations and unknowns does not prove that a nonlinear fluid problem has a unique smooth solution for all times. The Clay Mathematics Institute’s Navier–Stokes problem concerns existence and smoothness in specified three-dimensional settings. It is not the statement that no flow has an analytic solution. The falling film and channel below are explicit analytic solutions under their particular assumptions.

Special cases retain distinct hypotheses L58 23:35

Steady motion means ∂tv=0 at fixed spatial position. Convective acceleration v·∇v can remain nonzero. For example, the incompressible planar field v=(a x,−a y,0), with constant rate a, is steady but has acceleration (a²x,a²y,0). The derivative follows a particle through a spatially changing velocity field. A uniform translation, by contrast, has neither local nor convective acceleration.

Inviscid motion sets the viscous stress to zero, leaving Euler’s momentum equation. At an ideal solid wall, the usual inviscid boundary condition prevents penetration; a no-slip tangential condition cannot generally be carried over from the viscous boundary-value problem. The zero-viscosity limit can be singular because thin viscous layers can remain important near boundaries.

Irrotationality means ∇×v=0. On a sufficiently regular simply connected domain this permits a single-valued velocity potential v=∇φ. On a domain with holes, zero curl is only a local condition; a nonzero circulation around a hole can obstruct a global single-valued potential. Adding incompressibility gives ∇²φ=0 wherever the potential is valid. Neither inviscid nor incompressible alone guarantees irrotationality. This separates the assumptions that the lecture combines for potential-flow simplification.

The lecture problem: a uniform falling film L59 0:12

Let x2 point down a plane inclined by angle β to the horizontal, x3 point normally outward from the plane, and x1 be the spanwise coordinate. The film occupies0≤x3≤h, with constant thickness h>0 and uniform atmospheric pressure p0 on its flat upper surface. Gravitational acceleration has components (0,g sinβ,−g cosβ), where g>0. Density ρ and viscosity μ>0 are constant. The solid is stationary, the free surface has negligible tangential air stress, and surface curvature is zero.

Seek a steady translation-invariant, unidirectional laminar solution v=(0,u(x3),0). This is an ansatz for a symmetric solution branch, supported by the stated uniform geometry and loading. Infinite width by itself does not prove that every possible flow is spanwise-uniform or stable to disturbances. Within the ansatz, incompressibility is satisfied and convective acceleration vanishes: the nonzero velocity differentiates in x2, whereas u varies only in x3.

In the lecture’s staged derivation, one first assumes v1=v3=0 and independence of x1, leaving v2 as a function of x2,x3. Continuity then supplies ∂2v2=0. This identifies why the final profile depends only on the wall-normal coordinate. An independent velocity component or a varying free-surface height would change that reduction and require a different calculation.

Recover pressure and all stress components L59 11:37

The only nonzero entries of the stretching tensor are D23=D32=u′/2, where prime denotes d/dx3. Its trace is zero. The Cauchy stress therefore has diagonal entries−p and shear entries σ23=σ32=μu′. Stokes’ hypothesis is unnecessary for this incompressible calculation because the volumetric rate already vanishes.

\[ \boldsymbol\sigma=\begin{pmatrix} -p&0&0\\0&-p&\mu u'\\0&\mu u'&-p \end{pmatrix}. \tag{15.2} \]

The x1 momentum equation gives ∂1p=0. Normal momentum gives ∂3p=−ρg cosβ. Integrating in x3 allows an arbitrary function of the remaining coordinate, so initially p=−ρg cosβ x3+C(x2). The free-surface condition p(x2,h)=p0, with p0 and h constant, gives C=p0+ρgh cosβ. Thus

\[ p(x_3)=p_0+\rho g\cos\beta\,(h-x_3),\qquad\partial_2p=0. \tag{15.3} \]

The normal hydrostatic pressure variation coexists with downslope motion. Setting pressure equal to atmospheric everywhere would violate the normal momentum equation except in a special orientation. The pressure boundary condition, rather than a guess that a free-surface flow has uniform pressure, determines the integration function.

The velocity profile and its boundary conditions L59 30:27

Downslope momentum is μu″=−ρg sinβ. Its integral is u=−ρg sinβ x3²/(2μ)+A x3+B. The no-slip condition u(0)=0 gives B=0. Negligible upper tangential traction means σ23(h)=μu′(h)=0, giving A=ρgh sinβ/μ. Hence

\[ u(x_3)=\frac{\rho g\sin\beta}{2\mu}\,x_3(2h-x_3). \tag{15.4} \]

The profile is a concave parabola. Its slope vanishes at the free surface, not its value. Imposing zero upper velocity instead would solve a different two-solid-wall problem. The free surface moves downstream, while zero normal velocity and constant thickness satisfy its steady kinematic condition in this particular solution.

The surface speed is us=ρg sinβ h²/(2μ). The volume rate per unit span is Q′=∫0h u dx3=ρg sinβ h³/(3μ), so mean speed is Q′/h=2us/3. The wall shear magnitude is ρgh sinβ. For the fluid’s lower boundary, outward normal is−e3, so the wall exerts traction−ρgh sinβ e2 on the fluid. The fluid exerts the opposite downslope traction on the solid. This force pair provides an independent sign check.

For constructed inputs ρ=1000 kg/m³, μ=1 Pa s, g=10 m/s², h=0.002 m and β=30°, surface speed is0.01 m/s, mean speed is0.00666667 m/s and volume rate per width is0.0000133333 m²/s. Wall shear is10 Pa. The wall pressure exceeds p0 by17.320508 Pa. These values describe a synthetic Newtonian material and rounded gravity value selected for transparent arithmetic; they are not a property table for water.

Gravity power per unit bed area is ρg sinβ Q′. Integrating viscous dissipation across the film gives ∫0h μ(u′)²dx3=(ρg sinβ)²h³/(3μ), which is the same value. For the constructed inputs both equal0.0666667 W/m². No work enters through the stationary wall, and upper tangential stress is zero. This integrated equality checks the solution’s force and velocity signs without merely re-evaluating its differential equation.

A complete channel calculation

The channel is an original comparison problem. Its two tangential velocity conditions replace the film’s lower no-slip and upper zero-shear conditions. This change in boundary physics changes both the velocity profile and the stress distribution, even though the same linear viscous law and streamwise momentum operator are used.

Take a horizontal channel 0≤y≤h with stationary lower wall and upper wall moving at speed U along x. Let steady fully developed velocity be v=(u(y),0,0), constant μ>0 and a pressure gradient ∂xp=−G. Mass balance is satisfied identically and convective acceleration vanishes. Streamwise momentum becomes μu″=−G. Integrating twice and imposing u(0)=0,u(h)=U gives u(y)=Uy/h+G y(h−y)/(2μ).

Flow rate and shear signs

The volume rate per width is Q′=∫0h u dy=Uh/2+Gh³/(12μ). The stress component σxy=μu′ equals μU/h+G(h−2y)/2. At the lower boundary the outward normal of the fluid is −ey, so traction on the fluid has x component −σxy(0); at the upper boundary it is +σxy(h). The fluid’s force on either wall reverses the sign. Using a wall-normal derivative without stating which normal points outward can reverse the reported force.

Mechanical power and limiting cases

Multiplying μu″=−G by u and integrating gives μUu′(h)+GQ′=μ∫0h(u′)²dy. The left side is moving-wall power plus pressure-work input per channel length and width, with the stated signs. The right side is viscous dissipation. G=0 returns linear Couette flow; U=0 returns a parabola. An adverse pressure gradient can reverse velocity over part of the gap even while the net rate remains positive. This exact laminar solution does not establish its stability at arbitrary Reynolds number.

What a fluid problem still needs

A compressible calculation also needs thermal closure and an equation of state. An inviscid approximation changes admissible wall conditions; it cannot retain no-slip by assumption when viscosity has been removed. An irrotational velocity field is a separate kinematic restriction, not a synonym for inviscid flow. The governing equations, constitutive assumptions, domain, initial data and boundary conditions together define the problem.

The uniform film omits entrance development, side walls, surface deformation, capillarity and destabilizing disturbances. A small residual of its governing equations verifies the prescribed laminar solution, not its stability. A numerical method intended for a more general film should recover this analytic limit before being tested on changing film thickness or free-surface dynamics.

Executable boundary, flux and power checks

The program evaluates the film model with the constructed SI inputs. It checks the two distinct velocity boundary conditions, then uses numerical quadrature for flux and viscous power and compares them with independently derived integral expressions. Outputs are deterministic at the displayed precision. The program reads no files and does not solve a general free-surface problem.

import numpy as np

rho, mu, gravity, height = 1000.0, 1.0, 10.0, 0.002
beta = np.pi / 6.0
drive = rho * gravity * np.sin(beta)
y = np.linspace(0.0, height, 10001)
velocity = drive * y * (2.0 * height - y) / (2.0 * mu)
gradient = drive * (height - y) / mu
flux = np.trapezoid(velocity, y)
exact_flux = drive * height**3 / (3.0 * mu)
dissipation = np.trapezoid(mu * gradient**2, y)
gravity_power = drive * exact_flux
assert abs(velocity[0]) < 1e-14 and abs(gradient[-1]) < 1e-14
assert abs(flux - exact_flux) / exact_flux < 1e-8
assert abs(dissipation - gravity_power) / gravity_power < 1e-8
pressure_excess = rho * gravity * np.cos(beta) * height
print(f'surface speed: {velocity[-1]:.8f} m/s')
print(f'volume rate per width: {exact_flux:.10f} m^2/s')
print(f'wall shear magnitude: {drive * height:.6f} Pa')
print(f'wall pressure excess: {pressure_excess:.6f} Pa')
print(f'gravity power per bed area: {gravity_power:.8f} W/m^2')
print('boundary, flux and dissipation checks: PASS')

Exercises with worked solutions

1. Spherical stress. A symmetric tensor has every unit vector as an eigenvector. Prove that its three principal values are equal and explain the fluid-statics interpretation.

Solution

Choose its orthonormal principal basis with values a1,a2,a3. The vector(e1+e2)/√2 can be an eigenvector only if a1=a2; the pair(e2+e3)/√2 requires a2=a3. Hence σ=aI. If a=−p, every plane carries traction−pn and no shear. Having only three coordinate normals as eigenvectors would merely establish diagonality, not equality of all principal values.

2. Mechanical pressure. For μ=2 Pa s, λv=1 Pa s, thermodynamic pressure p=100 Pa and divv=3 s⁻¹, calculate bulk viscosity and mechanical pressure. Does the viscous stress have zero trace?

Solution

Kvis=1+4/3=7/3 Pa s. Thus pm=100−(7/3)3=93 Pa. The viscous-stress trace is3Kvis divv=21 Pa, so it is not deviatoric. The total stress trace is−300+21=−279 Pa, consistent with−trσ/3=93 Pa. A divergence-free test would not expose this distinction.

3. Nonzero steady acceleration. For v=(a x,−a y,0), find divergence, curl and acceleration. Which of steady, incompressible and irrotational imply zero acceleration?

Solution

The divergence is a−a=0. The curl is zero because the only spatial derivatives are diagonal. The field is time-independent, but v·∇v=(a²x,a²y,0). Therefore all three listed properties can hold simultaneously while acceleration is nonzero. Zero acceleration is an additional statement about the material derivative of velocity.

4. Replace the upper film boundary. In the falling-film momentum equation, replace zero upper shear by zero upper velocity. Find the profile and compare its rate with the free-surface result.

Solution

Integrate μu″=−A, where A=ρg sinβ, and impose u(0)=u(h)=0. The result is u=Ay(h−y)/(2μ), and Q′=Ah³/(12μ). The original free-surface rate is Ah³/(3μ), four times larger. The second stationary wall imposes a different physical constraint; its zero velocity is not an alternative expression for zero shear.

5. Add surface shear. Suppose air exerts uniform downslope shear s on the flat upper surface. Derive the new film profile and rate.

Solution

The upper outward normal is+e3, so σ23(h)=s gives μu′(h)=s. With u(0)=0, integration yields u=Ay(2h−y)/(2μ)+(s/μ)y. Its rate is Ah³/(3μ)+s h²/(2μ). Negative s opposes gravity and can reverse the surface or net flow at different thresholds. Pressure remains hydrostatic normal to the plane under the same unidirectional uniform-film assumptions.

6. Verify film power. Without numerical integration, show that gravitational work equals viscous dissipation for the zero-surface-shear film. State the units of the result.

Solution

Since u′=A(h−y)/μ, integrating μ(u′)² from0 to h gives A²h³/(3μ). Gravity power is A∫u dy=AQ′=A²h³/(3μ). The units are W/m², power per unit bed area. This equality concerns mechanical power within a constructed steady solution. It does not prove that the temperature remains uniform unless the thermal problem removes or redistributes the generated heat.

7. Global potential obstruction. In the plane excluding the origin, take v=(−y,x,0)/(x²+y²), with a constant circulation scale carrying the required units. Explain why zero curl away from the origin does not ensure a global single-valued velocity potential.

Solution

On a circle centered at the origin, velocity is tangent with magnitude1/r times the chosen circulation scale. Its line integral is2π times that scale. A single-valued potential would give zero integral around every closed loop. The nonzero circulation obstructs such a potential on the full punctured plane even though curl vanishes locally. A branch cut or a simply connected subdomain can permit a local potential.
