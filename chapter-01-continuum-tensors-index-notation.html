The continuum model, tensors and index notation

A temperature sensor returns one number. A force sensor must report a direction as well. A material that conducts heat differently along different axes requires a relation between two directions: the direction of the temperature gradient and the direction of heat flow. These are three different kinds of information. Confusing them creates errors before any differential equation is solved. This chapter develops the continuum idealization and the tensor notation needed to distinguish them, then uses explicit contractions to calculate a directional material response.

From particles to continuum fields L1 11:41

A rigid-body calculation determines translation and rotation while preserving distances between material points. That model cannot predict the bending of a beam or the changing shape of a fluid element. A deformable-body model must retain enough internal information to describe how neighboring material points move relative to one another. Continuum mechanics assigns physical quantities throughout a region of space and derives equations relating their changes to forces and material response.

A field assigns a value to each position and time in its domain. Let the spatial position be the vector \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^3\), measured in metres, and time be \(t\), measured in seconds. A temperature field is a function \(\theta(\mathbf x,t)\), with values in kelvins. A mass-density field \(\rho(\mathbf x,t)\) has units kg/m³. A velocity field \(\mathbf v(\mathbf x,t)\) has units m/s and gives a vector rather than a single real number. The type of the value and its dependence on position are separate properties.

The microscopic mass distribution of a collection of molecules does not resemble a smooth density field at arbitrarily small scales. To construct a local average, take a neighborhood of characteristic size \(\ell\) around \(\mathbf x\), with volume \(V_\ell\) and enclosed mass \(m_\ell\). The quotient \(m_\ell/V_\ell\) is a measured or computed average. A usable continuum field requires an intermediate range: \(\ell\) is large enough to average the microstructure and small enough to resolve the spatial variations of interest.

\[ \ell_{\mathrm{micro}}\ll\ell\ll L. \tag{1.1} \]

Here \(\ell_{\mathrm{micro}}\) denotes a relevant microscopic or microstructural length, while \(L\) denotes a macroscopic variation length. The two inequalities express different requirements. A large averaging volume suppresses molecular variability but can smear a thin layer that matters to the question. A small averaging volume resolves the layer but may no longer yield a reproducible average. The mathematical limit of a shrinking continuum element is taken within an idealized model; it is not an instruction to continue averaging below the atomic scale.

Bower, Applied Mechanics of Solids, §2.1, discusses continuum and Newtonian-frame idealizations and the length ranges over which they can approximate real matter. The averaging-scale inequality above is the present chapter’s explicit formulation. Continuum idealization does not imply that all physical fields are smooth everywhere. Interfaces, shocks and cracks need piecewise smooth fields, jump conditions or additional models. Continuum mechanics also does not require one homogeneous material throughout the entire body.

For a constructed counting example, suppose the number density is \(n=10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), uniform over a cube of side \(10^{-5}\) m. Its expected number of particles is \(N=n\ell^3=10^{10}\). If particle counts were independent Poisson samples, their relative standard deviation would be \(N^{-1/2}=10^{-5}\). That last estimate uses a statistical model; correlated molecules and heterogeneous microstructures need a different fluctuation analysis. A large count by itself does not establish local thermodynamic equilibrium.

For gases, the Knudsen number is \(\mathrm{Kn}=\lambda/L\), where \(\lambda\) is the molecular mean free path and \(L\) is a chosen length relevant to the phenomenon. Both have units of length, so the ratio is dimensionless. The lecture introduces small Knudsen number as a diagnostic for continuum modeling. A commonly used boundary such as 0.01 is a regime guideline, not a theorem guaranteeing a particular error. The relevant \(L\) may be a channel gap or a gradient length; choosing a large apparatus dimension can conceal rarefaction in a small gap.

Scalars, vectors and physical meaning L2 1:35

A scalar has one real value at a given event and does not acquire directional components when an orthonormal coordinate basis is rotated. Temperature is the running example. It can vary strongly in space and time while remaining a scalar. The word constant instead describes lack of variation in specified variables. Thus \(\theta(\mathbf x,t)=300\ \mathrm K+g x_1\), with \(g\) a temperature gradient in K/m and \(x_1\) measured in metres, is a scalar field that is spatially nonuniform.

Choose three mutually perpendicular unit vectors \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3\), oriented as a right-handed Cartesian basis. A vector \(\mathbf a\) can be written uniquely as

\[ \mathbf a=a_1\mathbf e_1+a_2\mathbf e_2+a_3\mathbf e_3, \qquad a_i=\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf e_i. \tag{1.2} \]

The dot denotes the Euclidean inner product, which measures the component of one vector along another. Each \(a_i\) is a real component in the selected basis. If \(\mathbf a\) is a force, those components are in newtons; if it is velocity, they are in m/s. Rotating the basis changes the component list. It does not change the physical force or velocity. The distinction between the vector and its coordinates will later determine the tensor transformation rule.

Magnitude and direction are useful geometric descriptions, but they do not by themselves establish vector behavior. A vector must obey the linear addition and scalar multiplication appropriate to its physical role. Finite rotations illustrate the danger: specifying an axis and angle does not make finite rotations ordinary additive vectors. The order of two finite rotations generally changes the result. Angular velocity and infinitesimal rotation have different mathematical behavior from a finite rotation operation. Detailed rotation matrices appear in chapter 3.

For vectors \(\mathbf a=(1,2,0)\) and \(\mathbf b=(3,-1,2)\), expressed in the same basis and with the same units, vector addition gives \(\mathbf a+\mathbf b=(4,1,2)\). Multiplying \(\mathbf a\) by a dimensionless scalar 2 gives \((2,4,0)\). Components add because the basis representation is unique: if \(c_i\mathbf e_i=d_i\mathbf e_i\), taking the dot product with each basis vector gives \(c_i=d_i\). The uniqueness, rather than the shape of an arrow in a drawing, licenses componentwise calculation.

What makes a second-order tensor L2 11:21

The running material model relates a temperature gradient \(\mathbf g\), in K/m, to a heat-flux vector \(\mathbf q\), in W/m². The gradient records directional rates of temperature change; the heat flux records the rate of heat transfer across unit area, with its direction indicating transfer. For this example, suppose a fixed linear map \(\mathbf K\) produces

\[ \mathbf q=-\mathbf K\mathbf g. \tag{1.3} \]

Linearity means \(\mathbf K(\alpha\mathbf a+\beta\mathbf b)=\alpha\mathbf K\mathbf a+\beta\mathbf K\mathbf b\) for real coefficients \(\alpha,\beta\) and input vectors in the same space. In a fixed Euclidean basis, such a linear map is a second-order tensor. Its nine components are \(K_{ij}=\mathbf e_i\cdot(\mathbf K\mathbf e_j)\): the second index chooses the input direction and the first chooses the output component. Their units here are W/(m K). This convention will also be used for other linear maps in this edition.

A matrix is a component representation of the map. The map is the geometric object. An arbitrary table of nine numbers, with no specified relation to a change of basis, is not sufficient to define a physical tensor. Conversely, the same tensor has many different matrix representations. Chapter 3 derives the transformation rule by requiring that the output vector calculated in either basis represent the same physical response.

Choose the constructed conductivity matrix \(\mathbf K=\operatorname{diag}(4,1,1)\) W/(m K), where \(\operatorname{diag}\) means a matrix with the stated diagonal entries and zero off-diagonal entries. For \(\mathbf g=(1,2,0)\) K/m, equation (1.3) gives \(\mathbf q=(-4,-2,0)\) W/m². The output is not parallel to \(-\mathbf g=(-1,-2,0)\). A single conductivity number cannot describe this response in every direction. The unequal diagonal entries encode direction-dependent response, or anisotropy, in the selected material axes.

For an isotropic example with \(\mathbf K=k\mathbf I\), where \(k\) is a scalar conductivity and \(\mathbf I\) the identity map, every input gives \(\mathbf q=-k\mathbf g\). Direction is then preserved up to reversal. This is an illustrative constitutive assumption, a material-specific relation, rather than a result of tensor algebra alone. Tensors describe both isotropic and anisotropic behavior; tensor notation does not choose the material law.

An unrestricted order-\(r\) tensor in three dimensions has \(3^r\) component positions. A scalar is order zero and has one; a vector is order one and has three; a second-order tensor has nine; a fourth-order tensor has 81. Component positions need not be independent. A symmetric second-order tensor has \(K_{ij}=K_{ji}\), leaving three diagonal and three off-diagonal independent values. Symmetry is an additional property, not part of the definition of every second-order tensor.

The counts also depend on dimension. An order-three tensor in a two-dimensional vector space has eight component positions. A three-dimensional body considered under a two-dimensional approximation still needs a statement of which components vanish or are constrained. Plane stress and plane strain are different such statements, developed in later chapters. Dropping the third coordinate without identifying the model can change the physics.

Summation is a rule for each term L3 1:57

From here onward, an index repeated exactly twice in a single multiplicative term is summed over its allowed values. Unless specified otherwise, every Cartesian index runs from 1 to 3. The convention removes a summation sign; it does not change the underlying operation. Thus

\[ a_i b_i=\sum_{i=1}^{3}a_i b_i =a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3. \tag{1.4} \]

The repeated label \(i\) is a dummy index, also called a contracted or summed index. Its name is local to the term. Replacing both occurrences by \(j\) gives the same scalar. It is comparable to changing the name of a loop variable in a computer program, provided the change does not collide with another active label.

In contrast, the index \(i\) in \(c_i=a_i+b_i\) is free. It denotes a family of three equations: one for each possible value of \(i\). A valid component equation has the same free indices in every term on both sides. The equation does not sum the three occurrences of \(i\) across its full printed length. The convention applies to each term separately. Thus \(a_i b_i+c_i d_i\) has two separate scalar sums and is valid.

For the running map, \(q_i=-K_{ij}g_j\) has free index \(i\) and dummy index \(j\). Fixing \(i=1\) gives \(q_1=-(K_{11}g_1+K_{12}g_2+K_{13}g_3)\). Fixing \(i=2\) gives a second equation, and fixing \(i=3\) gives a third. One free index remains after contraction, consistent with a vector output. No assumption of diagonal \(\mathbf K\) is needed for this formula.

Two independent dummy indices produce a double sum. The scalar \(a_iK_{ij}b_j\) means \(\sum_i\sum_j a_iK_{ij}b_j\) and has nine terms before any zero entries are used. For the running \(\mathbf K\) and \(\mathbf a=\mathbf b=\mathbf g\), it becomes \(4g_1^2+g_2^2+g_3^2=8\) W K/m³. Equation (1.3) therefore gives \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf g=-8\) W K/m³. The sign is consistent with heat transfer opposing the temperature gradient in this positive-conductivity model.

An index appearing three times in a term is invalid under this convention. For example, \(a_i b_i c_i\) must not silently be interpreted as a sum. If the intended operation really is \(\sum_i a_i b_i c_i\), write the explicit sum and declare the operation. Such a formula is not generally a coordinate-invariant scalar constructed from three polar vectors. The convention prevents ambiguous contractions; it does not prohibit other well-defined component calculations when they are stated explicitly.

Renaming dummy indices must avoid capture. In \(K_{ij}g_j\), replacing \(j\) by \(i\) would create three occurrences of \(i\), destroy the free output index, and change the meaning. A safe change uses an unused label: \(K_{ik}g_k\). Likewise, expanding \((a_i b_i)(c_j d_j)\) requires independent sums. Writing all four factors with index \(i\) incorrectly merges the two sums and violates the convention.

Trace, contractions and result type L3 21:40

For a second-order tensor \(\mathbf A\), the trace is the sum of diagonal components:

\[ \operatorname{tr}\mathbf A=A_{ii}=A_{11}+A_{22}+A_{33}. \tag{1.5} \]

There are no free indices, so this contraction gives a scalar. Repeating two component indices in one factor has the same summation meaning as repeating them across two different factors. For a third-order tensor \(C_{ijk}\), the contraction \(C_{iji}=C_{1j1}+C_{2j2}+C_{3j3}\) leaves one free index \(j\). The result is a vector. The order of a result follows its free indices, rather than the number of index marks printed before contraction.

For the constructed conductivity, \(K_{ii}=6\) W/(m K). A trace is not the entire tensor. Both \(\operatorname{diag}(4,1,1)\) and \(\operatorname{diag}(2,2,2)\) have trace 6, while they produce different fluxes for the same gradient. One scalar cannot retain all directional information carried by a second-order map. The trace becomes valuable as a particular invariant, whose basis independence is proved in chapter 3.

Index contractions also identify transposition. In \(A_{ij}b_j\), the summed label is the second index of \(A\). In \(b_jA_{ji}\), it is the first. Scalar multiplication of individual components commutes, but the two contractions generally differ. For a matrix with \(A_{12}=2\), \(A_{21}=0\), and all other entries zero, taking \(\mathbf b=\mathbf e_2\) gives \(A_{ij}b_j=(2,0,0)\), whereas \(b_jA_{ji}=(0,0,0)\). A physical stress convention must therefore specify which index denotes the normal and which denotes force direction before symmetry is used.

The Kronecker delta and the identity map L4 5:14

The Kronecker delta is defined by \(\delta_{ij}=1\) when the two specified index values are equal and \(\delta_{ij}=0\) otherwise. It represents the identity map in an orthonormal Cartesian basis. The orthonormality statement is \(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j=\delta_{ij}\). There is no summation in that statement unless a label is repeated within a term.

Its substitution property follows directly from an explicit sum:

\[ \delta_{ij}a_j=\sum_{j=1}^{3}\delta_{ij}a_j=a_i. \tag{1.6} \]

To see the mechanism, fix \(i=2\). The three terms are \(0a_1+1a_2+0a_3\). Only the component whose index equals the free index survives. This proves the identity action for each output component. The result generalizes to other tensors: \(\delta_{ij}A_{jk}=A_{ik}\), with two free indices \(i,k\), and \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk}=\delta_{ik}\).

The contraction \(\delta_{ii}\) is 3, not 1. The repeated index first creates the sum \(\delta_{11}+\delta_{22}+\delta_{33}\). Each summand equals 1. The statement that a single diagonal entry is 1 must not be substituted for the trace of the identity. In dimension \(d\), the same argument gives \(\delta_{ii}=d\).

Now derive the dot product from the basis representation rather than memorize its coordinate form. Write \(\mathbf a=a_i\mathbf e_i\) and \(\mathbf b=b_j\mathbf e_j\), choosing different dummy labels so they remain independent until the bases are contracted. Bilinearity gives

\[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b =a_i b_j(\mathbf e_i\cdot\mathbf e_j) =a_i b_j\delta_{ij}=a_i b_i. \tag{1.7} \]

The delta eliminates one sum and leaves the familiar three-term expression. If the basis were not orthonormal, its inner-product matrix would replace \(\delta_{ij}\). Cartesian formulas cannot be transferred unchanged to a curvilinear coordinate basis. This course starts in Cartesian coordinates so that the physical content can be separated from those additional geometric complications.

A systematic method for tutorial calculations L5 0:43

Before simplifying an indexed expression, identify its free labels and paired dummy labels. Check each multiplicative term independently. Expand one contraction if its meaning is uncertain. Apply a delta substitution only when the contraction it represents is valid. Finally, check that the result has the expected free indices and physical units. This procedure catches both syntactic and scientific mistakes.

For example, \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{ji}\) has two dummy labels. Eliminating the first delta gives \(\delta_{jj}=3\). In contrast, \(\delta_{ii}\delta_{kk}\) contains two separate traces, giving \(3\times3=9\). Both expressions display two deltas and four indices; their contraction patterns differ. The distinction becomes important when fourth-order elastic tensors are contracted later in the course.

A three-delta closed chain behaves similarly: \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk}\delta_{ki}=\delta_{ik}\delta_{ki}=\delta_{ii}=3\). The open chain \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk}\), however, equals \(\delta_{ik}\) and remains a second-order object. Closing its ends takes a trace. These are algebraic identities in a specified dimension, rather than special empirical properties of a material.

The ten tutorial problems also revisit the component count and the classification of scalars and vectors as tensors. Their common point is that representation and operation must be tracked together. A free index represents a direction still available in the output. A contraction removes a paired direction. That interpretation makes the notation useful when the expressions become longer than a few components.

Executable contraction checks

The program calculates the running flux using both explicit summation and matrix notation. It separately checks open and closed delta chains. The inputs are the fixed constructed quantities above. The program uses NumPy, reads no files, and writes only its printed checks. Assertions compare independently expressed calculations; they do not establish that a real material has the assumed conductivity.

import numpy as np

identity = np.eye(3)
conductivity = np.diag([4.0, 1.0, 1.0])
gradient = np.array([1.0, 2.0, 0.0])
flux = -conductivity @ gradient
indexed = -np.einsum('ij,j->i', conductivity, gradient)
assert np.allclose(flux, indexed)
assert np.allclose(identity @ conductivity, conductivity)
closed_chain = np.einsum('ij,jk,ki->', identity, identity, identity)
two_traces = np.einsum('ii,kk->', identity, identity)
assert closed_chain == 3.0 and two_traces == 9.0
assert float(flux @ gradient) == -8.0
print('flux:', flux.tolist())
print('trace:', float(np.trace(conductivity)))
print('closed delta chain:', float(closed_chain))
print('two independent traces:', float(two_traces))
print('flux dot gradient:', float(flux @ gradient))
flux: [-4.0, -2.0, 0.0]
trace: 6.0
closed delta chain: 3.0
two independent traces: 9.0
flux dot gradient: -8.0

A useful deliberate fault is to replace \(K_{ij}g_j\) by \(K_{ji}g_j\) for a nonsymmetric test matrix. A diagonal or symmetric test matrix cannot expose that mistake. The simple running conductivity therefore needs an additional nonsymmetric test when this code becomes a general-purpose contraction routine. Tests inherit the symmetries of their inputs unless those symmetries are deliberately broken.

What has been established

The continuum assumption licenses field descriptions over a scale range; it does not eliminate microstructure or guarantee a material model. Tensor order identifies the geometric type of an object, while index contraction identifies the type of an expression. The heat-flux example shows why a directional response requires a map between vectors. Coordinate transformations, alternating tensors and differential operators remain to be developed before these ideas can be applied to stresses and deformation.

Exercises with worked solutions

1. A scalar that varies. Let \(\theta(\mathbf x,t)=300\ \mathrm K+a x_1+b t\), with \(a=2\) K/m and \(b=0.5\) K/s. Find the temperature at \(x_1=3\) m, \(t=4\) s. Explain why it remains a scalar even though it varies.

Solution

Substitution gives \(300+2(3)+0.5(4)=308\) K. A scalar assigns one coordinate-independent value to the event. Changing the spatial basis changes the coordinates used to label that event but not the temperature there. The field is neither spatially nor temporally constant; neither kind of variation changes its tensor order.

2. Contract a third-order tensor. A constructed dimensionless tensor has components \(C_{ijk}=i+2j+3k\), where the numerical labels \(i,j,k\) take values 1, 2, 3 without summation in this component definition. Find \(b_j=C_{iji}\).

Solution

The definition is a rule for filling an array in one chosen basis; tensor behavior under another basis would require transforming that array. In the contraction, \(C_{iji}=\sum_i(4i+2j)=4(1+2+3)+6j=24+6j\). Thus \(\mathbf b=(30,36,42)\). The contracted label \(i\) disappears, while the free label \(j\) remains. Calling this expression a scalar because it contains a summation would lose its output direction.

3. Delta chains in arbitrary dimension. In a \(d\)-dimensional orthonormal space, calculate \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{ji}\), \(\delta_{ii}\delta_{jj}\), and \(\delta_{ij}\delta_{jk}a_k\).

Solution

The first contraction is \(\delta_{ii}=d\), since the identity has \(d\) diagonal entries. The second is the product of two independent traces, \(d^2\). In the third, summing \(j\) first gives \(\delta_{ik}a_k\), and summing \(k\) gives \(a_i\). Its free index \(i\) confirms a vector result. At \(d=3\), the scalar answers are 3 and 9.

4. Diagnose the notation. Assess the expressions \(c_i=A_{ij}b_j\), \(c_i=A_{ij}b_i\), and \(s=a_i b_i c_i\) under the stated summation convention. Repair the invalid ones without guessing their intended physics.

Solution

The first is valid: \(j\) is summed and \(i\) is free on both sides. In the second, the right side sums \(i\) and leaves \(j\) free, while the left side leaves \(i\) free. If a transposed-map output is intended, write \(c_j=A_{ij}b_i\), or rename the entire free label to obtain \(c_i=A_{ji}b_j\). The third repeats \(i\) three times, so it is invalid shorthand. An explicit component sum \(s=\sum_{i=1}^3 a_i b_i c_i\) defines one possible operation, but it must not be assumed to be a rotational scalar without further structure.

5. A directional material response. For \(\mathbf K=\operatorname{diag}(4,1,1)\) W/(m K), compare gradients \((1,0,0)\) and \((0,1,0)\) K/m. Then find a scalar conductivity that would match both fluxes if one exists.

Solution

The fluxes are \((-4,0,0)\) and \((0,-1,0)\) W/m². The first requires \(k=4\) and the second \(k=1\) in a scalar model \(\mathbf q=-k\mathbf g\). No single \(k\) satisfies both. Matching one loading direction does not identify a directional material law. The two measurements would already refute this particular scalar-conductivity hypothesis, although they would not identify all nine entries of a general linear map.

6. An averaging scale. In a constructed independent-Poisson particle model with \(n=10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), find the expected count and relative count standard deviation in cubes of side \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-6}\) m. Explain why these counts alone do not decide whether continuum flow equations are adequate.

Solution

The volumes are \(10^{-15}\) and \(10^{-18}\) m³, giving counts \(10^{10}\) and \(10^7\). Poisson standard deviation is \(\sqrt N\), so the relative values are \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-7/2}\), approximately \(3.16\times10^{-4}\). Reducing side length by ten reduces count by one thousand. Even if count fluctuations are small, a mean-free-path scale, correlation length, interface thickness, constitutive assumptions and the accuracy required for the intended observable can matter. These statistical calculations test an explicitly selected counting model.
